Modular Organization of Rba50 Reveals Distinct Domains 
for RNAPII Assembly and Interaction Network Formation

ABSTRACT
RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) is a conserved 12-­subunit enzyme essential for eukaryotic transcription. Although the structure 
and biological functions of RNAPII are well-­defined, the mechanisms by which its subunits are assembled into a functional 
complex remain only partially understood. Several RNAPII assembly factors have been identified, but the molecular principles 
by which they cooperate during polymerase biogenesis remain unclear. Rba50 is an essential, conserved RNAPII assembly factor 
implicated in multiple stages of polymerase biogenesis, yet its mode of action remains unknown. Here, we combine structural 
prediction, targeted interaction assays, and functional analyses to reveal a modular organization of Rba50 with separable activ­
ities. Rba50 comprises two functionally distinct but cooperative regions: a structured C-­terminal module that provides RNAPII 
assembly-­associated activity and an intrinsically disordered N-­terminal module that functions as a multivalent interaction hub. 
We map a direct interaction between the assembly factor Npa3 and the first N-­terminal disordered segment, residues 1–89, and 
show that the N-­terminal module expands the Rba50 interaction network. Functional assays indicate that the C-­terminal region 
rescues specific rba50-­3 phenotypes and supports proliferative recovery under permissive plasmid-­shuffle conditions, whereas 
robust Rba50 function also requires determinants outside the C-­terminal region. We further identify a CRM1-­dependent leucine-­
rich nuclear export signal within residues 239–249 that limits nuclear accumulation of Rba50 at steady state. Together, our 
results support a modular architecture in which distinct functional elements within Rba50 contribute to RNAPII biogenesis, 
interaction-­network formation, and nucleocytoplasmic distribution.
1 | Introduction
RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) is the central enzyme of eukary­
otic transcription [1–4]. It is composed of 12 subunits that assem­
ble into a conserved and highly structured complex [1, 3, 5, 6]. 
Although high-­resolution structures of RNAPII are available [3], 
the way through which its subunits are assembled remains only 
partially understood. Accumulating evidence indicates that 
RNAPII assembly requires a dedicated set of biogenesis factors, 
including Rba50, Gpn2, Gpn3, Npa3, Rtr1, and Bud27, which act 
at distinct stages of complex formation [7–17]. In both yeast and 
humans, Rba50 (RPAP1 in humans) has been shown to interact 
with several RNAP subunits, including Rpb10, Rpb2, Rpb3, and 
Rpb11 [14, 18–21], underscoring a conserved functional role.
Previous studies from our laboratory and others have outlined 
a working model in which Rba50 cooperates with Gpn2 to 
promote formation of the Rpb3 subcomplex (Rpb3–Rpb10–
Rpb11–Rpb12) [7, 22]. At later stages, Rba50 contributes to the 
incorporation of Rpb2 through coordinated action with Gpn2,

Rpc40 subcomplex and in the loading of the Rpc128 module

Despite its essential role, the molecular organization of Rba50 
and the basis by which it dynamically engages multiple partners 
remain poorly defined. Mechanistic studies have traditionally 
focused on folded domains, yet the structure of Rba50 has not 
been experimentally elucidated. In this study, we combined 
structural prediction, targeted interaction assays, and func­
tional analyses to reveal a previously unrecognized modular 
organization of Rba50.

2.3 | GST Pull-­Down Assays
GST-­Rba50, GST-­Rba50 (Δ1–89), and GST-­Rba50 (N-­ter1–89) were 
expressed in E. coli BL21 cells and purified by affinity chroma­
tography using glutathione-­Sepharose beads according to the 
manufacturer's protocol (GE Healthcare). Pull-­down assays 
were performed to assess physical interactions between Rba50 
(or deletion derivatives) and Npa3. Native protein extracts from 
the indicated yeast strain (YXQ19, Npa3-­13myc) were incubated 
with GST-­Rba50-­glutathione-­agarose beads or GST–glutathione-­
agarose beads at 4°C for 1 h. Beads were washed three times, and 
bound proteins were eluted by boiling in Laemmli sample buffer, 
resolved by SDS-­PAGE, and analyzed by Western blotting using 
anti-­Myc antibodies to detect Npa3-­13myc.
2.4 | Fluorescence Microscopy

iments, rba50Δ cells maintained by pRS316-­RBA50-­13myc were 
transformed with the indicated LEU2 plasmids and imaged after

cells expressing pRS425-­RBA50-­GFP were grown to logarith­
mic phase and treated with 100 ng/ml leptomycin B for 2 h be­

were acquired using a DeltaVision Elite microscope (Applied 
Precision Inc., Mississauga, ON, Canada) and processed with

4 | Discussion
In this study, we combined structural prediction, localization 
analyses, functional genetics, and interaction assays to define a 
previously unrecognized modular architecture of Rba50 and to 
dissect how its distinct regions contribute to RNAPII biogenesis. 
Although Rba50 has long been recognized as a core assembly 
factor for RNAPII and RNAPIII [7, 14], the molecular principles 
underlying its diverse functions have remained unclear. By in­
tegrating structural predictions and functional and interaction-­
based analyses, we uncover features of Rba50 that refine the 
current understanding of how polymerase assembly is coordi­
nated in eukaryotic cells.
Structural comparisons using the AlphaFold-­predicted model of 
Rba50 revealed similarity to several transport-­related proteins, 
including importins and factors involved in vesicular or endoso­
mal trafficking. The most prominent similarity was with Kap95, 
a principal receptor for classical nuclear import [32]. Although 
structural similarity does not imply functional equivalence, this 
overlap suggests that Rba50 may share structural features with 
transport regulators. Consistent with observations in mammals 
and plants, where RPAP1/IYO undergoes regulated nuclear ac­
cumulation during differentiation [24, 28], our results extend 
this observation to budding yeast, suggesting that Rba50 har­
bors an intrinsic nucleocytoplasmic shuttling capacity.
In this respect, we identified a CRM1-­dependent leucine-­rich 
NES in Rba50 and showed that two different leucine-­to-­alanine 
substitutions, L239A and L242A, resulted in pronounced nuclear 
accumulation of the protein. Pharmacological inhibition of 
Crm1 using LMB further caused nuclear enrichment of Rba50-­
GFP, demonstrating that Rba50 nuclear export depends on the 
classical exportin pathway. Notably, this altered distribution did 
not compromise cell viability, despite Rba50 being an essential 
factor for RNAPII assembly, a process proposed to occur in the 
cytoplasm prior to nuclear import [7, 14]. This apparent discrep­
ancy can be reconciled by considering the temporal and kinetic 
aspects of Rba50 function rather than its steady-­state distri­
bution. Thus, NES disruption may primarily increase nuclear 
retention without fully abolishing cytosolic availability. Even 
a small cytosolic pool of Rba50, perhaps derived from de novo 
synthesis, may be sufficient to sustain RNAPII assembly. Future 
work will aim to determine whether regulated nucleocytoplas­
mic shuttling of Rba50 becomes functionally important under 
conditions that impose increased demands on RNA polymerase 
II biogenesis, such as transcriptional stress.
Our work further shows that Rba50 consists of two function­
ally separable yet cooperative modules. The N-­terminal region, 
enriched in intrinsically disordered sequences (IDRs), provides 
the primary interaction surface for assembly factors and poly­
merase subunits. Consistent with the established role of IDRs as 
flexible, multivalent interaction platforms, residues 1–89 are re­
quired for binding to Npa3, and the broader N-­terminal domain 
co-­purifies with a wide array of Rba50-­interacting proteins 
in vivo, including associations with GPN-­family proteins and 
shared RNAP subunits such as Rpc40, Rpc19, and Rpb10. The 
N-­terminal region thus likely functions as an interaction hub 
through which Rba50 engages the RNAPII assembly network.
Interestingly, RNA polymerase I components, including Rpa190 
and Rpa49, were also recovered, suggesting a potential inter­
section between the Rba50 N-­terminal interactome and RNA 
polymerase I-­associated proteins, the functional significance of 
which remains to be explored.
In contrast, the C-­terminal region does not serve as a major 
interaction platform. Instead, it carries substantial activity re­
quired to restore RNAPII assembly-­associated phenotypes, as it 
rescues growth defects in rba50-­3 mutant cells, corrects Rpb1 
mislocalization, and supports colony formation after prolonged 
plasmid-­shuffle outgrowth under permissive conditions. These 
observations suggest that important activity required to support 
the assayed RNAPII biogenesis readouts resides within the C-­
terminal region, but they should not be interpreted as evidence 
that the isolated C-­terminal region is functionally equivalent to 
full-­length Rba50.
Although the C-­terminal region of Rba50 associates with far 
fewer proteins than the N-­terminal region in IP–MS analyses, 
the stable interactors detected are compatible with its ability to 
support partial growth recovery and selected RNAPII assembly-­
associated readouts. Notably, the C-­terminal fragment retains 
association with the RNAPII subunit Rpb2, a key substrate of 
Rba50-­dependent assembly, together with general chaperone 
and protein quality-­control factors such as Hsp70 family mem­
bers and Cdc48. This limited but functionally relevant inter­
action set is compatible with a model in which the C-­terminal 
region provides assembly-­associated activity. By contrast, the 
N-­terminal IDRs expand the interaction network, thereby

The plasmid-­shuffle growth assays should, therefore, be inter­
preted as assays of recovery under selection rather than as direct 
tests of equivalence to full-­length Rba50. This distinction is im­
portant because the recovered strains are not uncompensated 
rba50Δ null cells, but cells expressing plasmid-­borne Rba50 frag­
ments after loss of the URA3-­marked full-­length RBA50 plasmid. 
Thus, recovery after counterselection indicates that these frag­
ments retain activity above a minimal viability threshold under 
permissive conditions, not that they fully substitute for intact 
Rba50. Consistent with this interpretation, shorter C-­terminal 
fragments beginning at residues 202 or 203 could not be recovered 
after counterselection, indicating that fragment-­supported recov­
ery is boundary-­dependent rather than a nonspecific consequence 
of expressing any C-­terminal segment. Growth recovery can also 
be influenced by incubation time, plasmid copy number, selection 
conditions, residual protein during early counterselection, and 
slow outgrowth of cells that maintain sufficient fragment activ­
ity. Accordingly, the prolonged-­incubation assay and the shorter 
assay format report different aspects of fragment-­supported recov­
ery: eventual colony formation versus early growth kinetics. Thus, 
the C-­terminal region retains activity sufficient to support recov­
ery under permissive plasmid-­shuffle conditions, whereas robust 
Rba50 function depends on additional determinants present in 
the intact protein.
This modular organization suggests an evolutionary logic in 
which a conserved C-­terminal region provides a core assembly-­
associated activity, whereas the flexible and expandable N-­
terminal IDRs confer adaptability by mediating species-­specific 
or condition-­specific interactions. Such an arrangement would 
allow RNAPII biogenesis to be tuned in response to cellular 
or environmental demands, balancing structural conservation 
with regulatory plasticity across eukaryotes. Comparative anal­
yses across eukaryotes may reveal how the balance between 
structural conservation and IDR-­driven plasticity has shaped 
the control of polymerase biogenesis.
Although our genetic and cell-­biological data indicate that the C-­
terminal region of Rba50 is sufficient to restore selected RNAPII 
assembly-­associated phenotypes, we have not directly measured 
the formation of the Rpb2–Rpb3 subcomplex in the presence 
of individual Rba50 fragments. Direct biochemical assays of 
Rpb2–Rpb3 association will be important in future work to de­
fine precisely how each Rba50 module contributes to distinct 
steps of RNAPII assembly. Future work will also need to define 
high-­resolution structures of Rba50 and its complexes with Gpn2, 
Npa3, and RNAPII subunits to clarify how its two modules co­
operate and how transport-­associated elements interface with its 
assembly role. Together, our results provide a framework linking 
structural prediction, modular organization, nucleocytoplasmic 
transport, and IDR biology to explain how Rba50 contributes to 
RNAPII assembly.
