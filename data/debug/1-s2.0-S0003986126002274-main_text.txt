A B S T R A C T
The Polycomb repressive complexes can be considered a paradigm of chromatin-based gene regulation in ani­
mals. Two of the proteins involved in such complexes are the Ring finger protein 1 (RING1) and the intrinsically 
disordered protein RING1A and YY1 binding protein (RYBP), which binds to RING1 in some of the Polycomb 
complexes. Peptidyl arginine iminohydrolases (PADIs) are enzymes involved in the post-translational modifi­
cation of arginine to citrulline. PADI4, one of the five isoforms of PADI in humans, citrullinates RING1B and 
RYBP at specific arginines at their C-terminal (Arg231) and N-terminal (Arg53) regions, respectively. In this 
work, we studied in vitro and in silico whether isolated peptides from each protein, encompassing those wild-type 
arginines and their citrulline-modified counterparts, were capable of binding to PADI4. The isolated peptides, 
either in wild-type or modified form, were monomeric and disordered as indicated by far-UV CD and NMR. The 
affinity constants of peptides for PADI4, as measured by fluorescence, biolayer interferometry (BLI) and 
isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), were in the low micromolar range (dissociation constants around 10- 
20 μM), and the affinities were smaller for the citrullinated variants. Molecular docking simulations showed that 
the isolated peptides from each protein, either in wild-type form or modified variants, targeted the active site of 
PADI4, corresponding to the catalytic residue Cys645. These findings suggest that the isolated peptides were 
capable of binding to the enzyme with affinities similar to those of intact RING1B or RYBP, and that citrullination 
of the arginines diminished the affinity for the enzyme.
1. Introduction
Specific and unique gene expression programs are necessary for 
successful development of multicellular organisms. Deciphering these 
intricate routes is currently under way, and results so far have suggested 
that histone and chromatin post-translational modification (PTM) 
contribute to modulate transcription and gene expression [1–3]. Poly­
comb systems comprise several arrays of multi-protein complexes, 
which are involved in: (i) catalyzing histone modification; and (ii) in­
teractions with nucleosomes, supporting mainly transcription repression 
of several genes as well as, in a few cases, an increase in gene activation 
[4–9].

Polycomb genes were firstly discovered over 70 years ago in 
Drosophila melanogaster, where they are needed for repression of the 
homeotic (Hox) genes, which are involved in body plan specification 
[10]. The mammalian orthologues are crucial in modulating gene 
expression during development [11]. Polycomb proteins assemble into 
one of two large multi-protein complexes capable of modifying histones. 
The Polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase 
capable of mono-ubiquitylating histone H2A at Lys119 [12–15] 
(yielding H2AK119ub1). On the other hand, the Polycomb repressive 
complex 2 (PRC2) is capable of mono-, di- and tri-methylate histone H3 
at Lys27 [16,17]. Both PRC1 and PRC2 form the so-called Polycomb 
chromatin domains, which are formed by chromatin and specially 
enriched in H2AK119ub1, H3K27me3 and the Polycomb complexes 
themselves [18,19].
The catalytic core of the E3-ubiquitine ligase PRC1 is formed by 
RING1B (UniProt code: Q99496) – or alternatively its paralogue 
RING1A – and one of the six Polycomb group ring finger (PCGF) pro­
teins. The RING1 and PCGF proteins have a similar domain arrange­
ment: an N-terminal RING domain and a C-terminal RING-finger and 
WD40-associated ubiquitin-like (RAWUL) domain [20]. To ubiq­
uitinate the nucleosomes, RING1 and PCGF proteins hetero-dimerize 
through their RING domains, allowing interaction with an E2 conju­
gating enzyme [20–23]. The RAWUL domain binds to a range of other 
auxiliary proteins, which are selected by the PCGF components to form 
specific PRC1 complexes [20]. These auxiliary proteins are capable of: 
(i) modulating the PRC1 catalytic activity, and (ii) targeting a specific 
site in the genome [24,25]. These complexes are named either canonical 
PRC1 (cPRC1) or non-canonical PRC1 (ncPRC1). The cPRC1 complexes 
arrange around either PCGF2 or PCGF4 and are constituted by one of the 
five chromodomain-containing paralogues (CBX2, 4, 6, 7 or 8), which 
are associated to the RAWUL domain of RING1, and a polyhomeotic 
subunit (PHC1, 2 or 3) [20,24]. On the other hand, the ncPRC1 com­
plexes arrange around any of the six PCGF (PCGF1 to PCGF6), and they 
include RING1, as well as the intrinsically disordered RING1A and 
YY1-binding protein (RYBP, Uniprot: Q8N488) – or alternatively, its 
paralogue YY1-associated factor – which binds to the RAWUL domain of 
C-RING1B [26]. They also include various additional auxiliary proteins 
that are dependent on the type of PCGF protein present in the complex 
[25,27–29].
Citrullination is a PTM occurring in the presence of Ca2+ and cata­
lyzed by L-arginine iminohydrolases (PADIs), a family of proteins also 
known as peptidyl-arginine deiminases (EC 3.5.3.15). PADI4 (Uniprot 
code: Q9UM07) is one of the five isoforms of L-arginine iminohydrolases 
(EC 3.5.3.15) found in humans, which convert arginine into citrulline, 
by deamination of its side-chain [30,31]. Among other cell locations, 
PADI4 is usually found in the cytoplasmic granules of neutrophils, eo­
sinophils or macrophages [30]. PADI4 is also involved in the citrulli­
nation of histones H1 [30], H3 [32], H2A/H2B dimers [33] and H4 [34], 
and it is capable of counteracting histone arginine methylation [35]. We 
have recently reported that PADI4 binds to RYBP [36] and the RAWUL 
domain of RING1B, C-RING1B (residues 228-336 of the intact protein) 
[37], modifying some of the arginines in those polypeptides. In RYBP, 
the arginine post-translationally modified by PADI4 seems to be Arg53, 
although Arg56 is also very close, both being located at its N-terminus 
[36]; and, in C-RING1B, the modified arginine is Arg231 [37].
In this work, we wondered whether the isolated regions around those 
arginine residues of RYBP and C-RING1B polypeptides were also capable 
of binding to PADI4. To answer those questions, we synthesized two 
peptides for C-RING1B (the wild-type and the citrulline-modified one at 
Arg231), and three peptides for RYBP (the wild-type, the citrullinemodified at Arg53, and the citrulline-modified at Arg56 [36]). We first 
characterized the conformational features of these isolated peptides by 
diffusion ordered spectroscopy (DOSY), 1D- and 2D-1H NMR, and we 
found that all of them were disordered and monomeric in aqueous so­
lution. To assess peptide binding to PADI4, we used fluorescence, 
isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) and biolayer interferometry (BLI); 
the three techniques showed binding for all peptides to PADI4. 
Furthermore, the wild-type peptides derived from C-RING1B and RYBP 
were citrullinated by PADI4 and, therefore, they were substrates, as 
observed by an enzymatic colorimetric assay (COLDER). Molecular 
simulations could reproduce the general trend observed in the experi­
ments in vitro, and provided the additional information that the cit­
rullinated peptides targeted the catalytic site of PADI4, as well. In 
general terms, we can conclude that the citrullinated peptides showed a 
slightly lower affinity for PADI4 than the corresponding wild-type ones.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials
Imidazole, Trizma base, Kanamycin, DNase, 3-(trimethylsilyl) pro­
pionic acid-2,2,3,3-2H4-sodium salt (TSP), SIGMAFAST protease inhib­
itor tablets, Ni2+-resin, glycerol, N-α-Benzoyl-L-arginine ethyl ester 
(BAEE), L-citrulline, 3-hydroxyimino 2-butanone (diacetyl monoxime), 
thiosemicarbazide (TSC), SO4H2 (98 %), PO4H3 (85 %), NH4Fe 
(SO4)2 × 12H2O, GSK484 (an inhibitor of PADI4), NaCl, antipyrine and 
ultra-pure dioxane were purchased from Sigma (Madrid, Spain). The 
β-mercaptoethanol was from BioRad (Madrid, Spain). Isopropyl-β-D-1- 
thiogalactopyranoside were obtained from Apollo Scientific (Stockport, 
UK). The detergent Triton X-100, the reducing agent TCEP (tris(2-car­
boxyethyl)phosphine), and the SDS protein marker (PAGEmark 
Tricolor) were from VWR (Barcelona, Spain). Amicon centrifugal de­
vices with a molecular weight (MW) cut-off of 50 kDa were from Milli­
pore (Barcelona, Spain). The rest of materials were of analytical grade. 
Water was deionized and purified on a Millipore system.
2.2. Protein expression and purification
The dimeric protein PADI4 (with 663 residues per monomer) was 
also purified as previously described [38]. PADI4 concentrations were 
determined by ultraviolet (UV) absorbance, employing an extinction 
coefficient at 280 nm estimated from the number of tyrosines and 
tryptophans (10 tryptophans and 13 tyrosines per monomer, ε280 
nm = 73540 M−1 cm−1) [39].
2.3. Synthesis of the wild-type and citrullinated variants of peptides 
derived from C-RING1B and RYBP proteins
Based on previous results on how the C-RING1B and the intact RYBP 
were capable of binding to PADI4 [36,37], we synthesized five peptides, 
whose sequences are reported in Table 1. The peptides related to 
C-RING1B were: the wild-type (C-RING-wt), and the modified one with a 
citrulline at Arg231 (C-RING-R231). The RYBP-derived peptides were: 
the wild-type (RYBP-wt), the modified peptide with a citrulline at Arg53 
(RYBP-R53), and the other one with a citrulline at position Arg56 
(RYBP-R56), which is very close to the former position but less favored 
in the binding to PADI4 [36]. All the peptides were synthesized as 
acetylated and amidated, to avoid possible structural fraying at both 
termini. We also added in the RYBP-derived peptides a tyrosine at the 
C-terminal end to allow for the quantitative determination of their 
concentration in solution, by employing UV absorption at 280 nm [39]. 
The concentration of the C-RING1B peptides was determined from the 
absorbance at 280 nm of the sole phenylalanine present in their 
sequence (ε280nm = 700 M−1 cm−1 [42]). All the peptides were produced 
by GenScript (Leiden, Netherlands), with a purity larger than 95 %.

2.5. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy
The NMR experiments on the C-RING1B or RYBP peptides were 
performed at 10 ◦C on a Bruker Avance 500 MHz spectrometer (Bruker 
GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany), equipped with a triple resonance probe 
and z-pulse field gradients. Spectra were processed with Bruker TopSpin 
2.1 (Bruker GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany). All NMR experiments were 
carried out in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, at pH 7.2, in the pres­
ence of 50 μL of D2O. Spectra were calibrated with TSP, by considering 
pH-dependent changes of its chemical shifts [47]; probe temperature 
was calibrated with methanol [47].
2.5.1. 1D-1H NMR spectra
A number of 128 scans were acquired with 16 K acquisition points for 
the homonuclear 1D-1H NMR spectra of each peptide at ~1.2 mM con­
centration. Water signal was suppressed with the WATERGATE 
sequence [48]. The spectra were processed after zero-filling (with a total 
matrix size of 32 K), apodised with an exponential window and base-line 
corrected.
2.5.2. Translational diffusion NMR (DOSY)
The peptide concentrations in DOSY experiments [49] were 
~100 μM, and 128 scans were performed, with a total amount of data of 
16 K in the F2 dimension. Measurements of the translational 
self-diffusion coefficient, D, were performed with the pulsed-gradient 
spin-echo sequence in the presence of 100 % D2O. Details on the 
experimental conditions, calibration of the gradient strength and fitting 
of the resulting curves have been described elsewhere [40]. The esti­
mated values of the hydrodynamic radii, Rh, of the peptides were 
determined from a comparison with the Rh of dioxane added to the so­
lution [40].
2.5.3. 2D-1H NMR spectra
Two-dimensional spectra of the C-RING1B and RYBP peptides were 
acquired in each dimension in phase-sensitive mode by using the timeproportional phase incrementation technique [50] and a spectral 
width of 5500 Hz; peptide concentration was the same used for the 
1D-1H NMR spectra. Standard TOCSY (with a mixing time of 80 ms) [51] 
and NOESY experiments (with a mixing time of 250 ms) [52] were 
performed by acquiring a data matrix size of 4096 × 512 points. The 
DIPSI (decoupling in the presence of scalar interactions) spin-lock 
sequence [53] was used in the TOCSY experiments with a relaxation 
time of 1 s. A number of 96 scans were acquired per increment in the first 
dimension. NOESY spectra were collected with 128 scans per increment 
in the first dimension with a relaxation time of 1 s. In both spectra, re­
sidual water signal was removed by using the WATERGATE sequence 
[48]. Data were zero-filled, resolution-enhanced with a square sine-bell 
window function optimized in each spectrum, and baseline-corrected. 
The 1H resonances were assigned by using the standard sequential 
assignment method [54]. The chemical shift values of Hα protons in 
random-coils were calculated by considering the neighboring residue 
effects [54–56].
2.6. Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC)
The binding affinities for the RING and RYBP peptides interacting 
with PADI4 were determined in a PEAQ-ITC titration calorimeter 
(MicroCal, Malvern-Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The titrations were car­
ried out in buffer 50 mM Tris at pH 8.0, at a temperature of 25 ◦C. 
Protein solutions in the cell at 5-20 μM were titrated with each of the 
peptide solutions at 50-200 μM from the injection syringe. In each 
experiment a sequence of nineteen injections of 2 μL of titrant solution 
spaced evenly over 150 s was programmed, with a stirring speed of 
750 rpm and a reference power of 10 μcal/s. Experiments were repeated 
twice. Additional experiments employing GSK484, a well-known 
orthosteric PADI4 inhibitor, were performed to assess whether the 
peptides compete for the binding to the active site. In this case, GSK484 
was added to the calorimetric cell solution at a concentration of 500 μM.
The binding isotherms were obtained by integrating the heats per 
injections in the thermograms and normalizing by the amount of ligand 
injected per injection. Data analysis was performed by non-linear leastsquares regression data analysis employing a user-defined fitting routine 
in Origin 7.0 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA) and considering a single 
binding-site model. The equilibrium association constant, Ka (and the 
dissociation constant, Kd = 1/Ka), and the apparent binding stoichiom­
etry, n, were estimated.

2.8. Biolayer Interferometry (BLI)
2.8.1. Experimental design
The association (kon) and dissociation (koff) rate constants of the 
binding reactions of the Polycomb peptides to PADI4 were determined 
by using a BLItz system (ForteBio, Pall, Barcelona, Spain) [63–67]. The 
buffer used in the experiments was that recommended by the manu­
facturer (Octet kinetics Buffer 10 × (10 × KB), PN 18-1105, Sartorius). 
As PADI4 had a His-tag, it was immobilized on His-tag biosensors (Octet 
NTA Biosensors, PN 18-5101) at 0.3 μM (in protomer units). Before the 
loading, each Polycomb peptide was tested for binding at the largest 
concentration assayed against unloaded tips; in all the cases, we did not 
observe any binding. The C-RING1B and RYBP peptide concentrations 
were in the range from 20 to 120 μM during the association step. The 
general scheme of the protein-association/dissociation reactions was: 
30 s of initial baseline acquisition with the 10 × kinetics buffer; 120 s of 
loading PADI4 into the His-tag biosensor; 30 s of baseline acquisition 
with the 10 × kinetics buffer; 120 s of association of each Polycomb 
peptide to the biosensor-immobilized-PADI4; and 120 s of dissociation 
of the corresponding peptide from the biosensor-immobilized-PADI4. 
Baseline measurements in the presence of unloaded tips were sub­
tracted from the measurement taken with the loaded tip. Experiments 
were repeated twice with new biosensors for each of the peptides.

2.9. Molecular docking simulations
Molecular docking simulations were carried out to study the binding 
locations of the C-RING1B and RYBP peptides on PADI4. The peptides 
were built by using UCFS Chimera [68], with acetylated and methylated 
capping at their N and C terminus, respectively. The structure of PADI4 
was build starting from its crystallographic conformation (PDB entry: 
3APN [69]), and missing loop portions were modeled as previously 
described [38]. To reduce the computational cost, a single protein 
monomer (663 residues) of PADI4 was used in the docking experiments. 
The software PyMol, version 2.1.1 (https://www.pymol.org) was used 
to display the docking poses.
The large number of rotational degrees of freedom of the peptides 
(78 rotatable bonds for C-RING-wt and its citrulline-modified counter­
part, and 82 for RYBP-wt and its two citrullinated ones) precluded the 
use of many long-established docking algorithm (e.g., 32 rotatable 
bonds is the limit considered reliable for AutoDock Vina [70]. For this 
reason, the generative diffusion model DiffDock was used [71], which 
uses a three-layer graph neural network to score potential docking poses 
and rank them according to a confidence value. To allow for a direct 
comparison with the binding score of other peptides we previously 
proved to bind PADI4 in experiments and simulations [61,62,72], 
default docking parameters were used for DiffDock: 20 diffusion time 
divisions, 18 actual inference steps, and no final step noise. Ten simu­
lation runs were performed and, among the top-ranked docking poses, 
the five most favorable ones were considered.
Additional runs were made to reduce the computational complexity 
of the simulations, by considering: (i) the peptides shortened by three 
residues on each termini (resulting in 54 rotatable bonds for each of the 
five peptides); and (ii) the sole PADI4 domain where docking poses 
tended to accumulate (residue 295−663 of the protein monomer).
3. Results
3.1. Conformational features of the C-RING1B and RYBP peptides
To perform the characterization of the conformational features of the 
C-RING1B and RYBP peptides (Table 1), we carried out 1D- and 2D- 1H 
NMR studies. We did not use fluorescence to characterize the structural 
features of the isolated peptides, since: (i) to allow for measurement of 
their concentration, the RYBP peptides had a non-natural tyrosine at the 
C-terminal region, whose maximum wavelength in the fluorescence 
spectrum does not change in the presence of well-defined or rigid 
structure [42]; and (ii) the C-RING1B ones had a sole phenylalanine.
The C-RING1B and RYBP peptides were monomeric, as concluded 
from: (i) the value of D measured by the DOSY experiment (Table 1); and 
(ii) the estimated Rh obtained from the comparison with the experi­
mentally measured D of dioxane by using the Stokes-Einstein relation­
ship [40]. In fact, the estimated values of Rh were similar to those 
obtained theoretically for random-coil polypeptides [41] with the same 
MWs (Table 1).
The disordered character of the Polycomb peptides was evident from 
the 1D-1H NMR spectra (Fig. 1), which showed a clustering of the signals 
of all the methyl protons of Leu, Ile and Val residues at ~0.8-1.0 ppm 
[54]. Similarly, the amide protons were clustered between 8.0 and 
8.6 ppm in all peptides (Fig. S1). These values for the methyl and amide 
protons are typical of residues belonging to disordered chains [54].
To further confirm the disordered nature of the C-RING1B and RYBP 
peptides, we also carried out 2D-1H NMR experiments. The peptides 
were found to be mainly disordered in solution, as suggested by two 
pieces of evidence. First, the sequence-corrected conformational shifts 
(Δδ) of Hα protons of all peptides for unambiguously assigned residues 
were within the commonly accepted range for random-coil peptides 
M.G. ́Alvarez-Rodríguez et al. 
Archives of Biochemistry and Biophysics 785 (2026) 110955 
4 

(Δδ ≤0.1 ppm) (Tables ST1 to ST5). And second, no long- or mediumrange NOEs were detected, but only sequential ones (i.e., αN (i, i+1) 
and βN (i, i+1)) in the polypeptide patches fully assigned (Fig. 2). It is 
interesting to note that citrullination did not affect the chemical shifts of 
backbone or side-chain protons (Tables ST1 to ST5).
It is important to indicate that the region around Arg231 in CRING1B appears disordered in the X-ray structure of this domain (PDB 
code: 3H8H [73]). Furthermore, the region around Arg53 and Arg56 in 
RYBP in the cryo-electron microscopy structure of a complex with other 
proteins show that both arginines also belong to a disordered 
polypeptide patch (PDB code: 8PP6 [15]). Therefore, the conformation 
of the isolated peptides obtained in our experiments was no more or­
dered than the one they have in the intact polypeptide chains.
Then, we can conclude that all isolated C-RING1B and RYBP peptides 
in solution were monomeric and disordered.

affinity constant to compare with those obtained by the other two 
techniques, by assuming a two-state process for the binding of each 
peptide to the enzyme. It is important to pinpoint that, in the context of 
the fluorescence experiments, we are assuming that the unbound and the 
free species were the only ones significantly populated (Eq. (1)). How­
ever, in the context of the BLI experiments, if we assume that there are 
more than those two species (that is, an intermediate state may exist 
between the free and bound states) the equilibrium dissociation constant 
cannot be obtained as Kd = koff/kon, but rather by using more complex 
relationships [74].
We first carried out fluorescence titrations with each of the peptides 
and PADI4. We observed a decrease of the fluorescence intensity as the 
concentration of the peptide was increased (by following the titration at 
either 280 or 295 nm) at any of the explored emission wavelengths. In 
this way, we were able to determine a Kd value for each peptide (Fig. 3
and S2, and Table 2). In the case of C-RING1B peptides, as they only 
contained a phenylalanine residue, the variation in the fluorescence 
intensity indicated that some of the tryptophans (10) and/or tyrosines 
(13) present in PADI4 were involved in the binding to the peptide. For 
the RYBP peptides, since they only contained an additional tyrosine at 
their corresponding C-terminal region, and variations in fluorescence 
intensity were also observed during the titration, then, these results 
suggested that some of the tryptophan residues of PADI4 were involved 
in the binding to these peptides.
The calorimetric titrations of the Polycomb peptides provided direct 
evidence for their interaction with PADI4 (Table 3, Fig. 4). The binding 
affinity of the unmodified C-RING1B and RYBP peptides was in the low 
micromolar range, similar to other previously characterized noncitrullinated peptides [61,62,72]. The citrullination of particular argi­
nines caused a decrease in the binding affinity; interestingly, the most 
significant change was observed in the RYBP-R56 peptide. Additional 
experiments performed by adding GSK484, an orthosteric inhibitor of 
PADI4, indicated that occupation of the active site in PADI4 precluded 
the interaction with the wild-type peptides (Fig. S3); therefore, the 
Polycomb wild-type peptides must bind to the active site of PADI4. 
Although this piece of evidence by itself cannot rule out the possibility of 
GSK484 binding to PADI4 and inducing a conformational change that 
hindered the interaction with the peptide in an allosteric fashion, the 
fact that wild-type peptides could be citrullinated by PADI4 confirmed 
the hypothesis that they bind to the active site (see below in this 
section).
Finally, we carried out BLI experiments to have another independent 
estimate of the affinity of the binding of the peptides to PADI4 (Table 4, 
Fig. 5 and S4). It is important to indicate that due to the immobilization 
of PADI4 to the biosensor, some of the potential peptide-binding regions 
in the enzyme molecules could get occluded, hampering the peptide 
binding. Although we acknowledge such limitation that constitutes a 
well-known drawback of this technique [63–66], in our case we wanted 
to have an additional method to further confirm the binding of the 
peptides to the enzyme together with fluorescence (which may not yield 
reliable affinity constants when there is a lack of structural changes 
around a tryptophan and/or tyrosine) or ITC (whose results may be 
difficult to interpret when there is a lack of a significant heat 
exchanged). In general terms, for both types of the Polycomb peptides, 
the values of the koff rates were larger when citrulline was present, 
compared to those for the wild-type species (Table 4). These findings 
resulted in larger dissociation constants for the citrullinated species from 
both Polycomb peptides than for the wild-type ones. Furthermore, in the 
case of the RYBP peptide citrullinated at position 56, the value of kon was 
much smaller (one order of magnitude) than for the peptide citrullinated 
at position 53 (Table 4), even though the value of the koff was similar for 
both modified peptides (~0.50 s−1). This suggests that, from a kinetic 
point of view, the binding of a modified arginine at position 56 is dis­
favored with respect to modification at position 53.
Then, all in all, although the particular PADI4-peptide binding af­
finities determined by the different techniques somewhat differ, they 
showed a common trend: dissociation constants in the low micromolar 
range for the unmodified peptides and larger dissociation constants for 
the citrullinated species, in both Polycomb peptides, compared to the 
wild-type ones.

3.3. All polycomb peptides targeted the catalytic active site of PADI4
Whereas the colorimetric assays provided a proof of the binding to 
the catalytic active site for the two wild-type Polycomb peptides (i.e., 
RING1B-wt and RYBP-wt), for the citrulline-modified peptides we 
lacked an analogous evidence. The presence of an allosteric rather than 
orthosteric binding site might be suspected in this case, because disso­
ciation of a citrullinated site occurs naturally after catalysis; therefore, 
affinity towards the active site could be comparatively less favorable 
than in the presence of a native arginine. With the aim of getting further 
details on the binding propensity of our Polycomb peptides towards 
PADI4, we performed molecular docking simulations by using the 
diffusion-based algorithm DiffDock [71].

and citrullinated species. In fact, confidence scores of −3.7 ± 0.3 were

shorter citrullination-prone peptides (15 or 16 residues long) derived 
from the ubiquitin ligase MDM2 [61].

and employing as docking host the C-terminal PADI4 domain (residue

were in this case more favorable (Table ST6), about −2.6. ± 0.2, and

peptides, including in some cases direct contact with the arginine side

one arginine (for RYBP-wt) or a competition between the arginine and 
citrulline (for RYBP-R53 and RYBP-R56 peptides) was observed.
4. Discussion
We have previously shown that the C-RING1B and RYBP intact 
polypeptides were capable of binding to PADI4, with affinity constants 
in the order of 10 μM (from the fluorescence measurements) and 1.5 μM 
(from the point of view of ITC) [36,37]. In this work, we showed that the 
isolated regions of each of those proteins, which are citrullinated by 
PADI4, were also capable of binding to PADI4 with affinity constants 
similar to those of the corresponding parental proteins, as obtained by 
fluorescence (Table 2) and ITC (Table 3). Furthermore, as it happens 
with the intact polypeptides, our colorimetric assays provided a direct 
proof that the wild-type peptides were substrates of the enzymes 
(Fig. S5); it is interesting to note that ITC experiments in the presence of 
GSK484 (an inhibitor of PADI4) also suggested that the peptides aimed 
to the active site. These results suggest that the isolated peptides could 
be used as competitors of the interactions of the corresponding parental 
proteins with PADI4. Furthermore, keeping in mind that the isolated 
peptides could have different behavior to that of the corresponding 
polypeptide region within the intact proteins, they might be used as the 
starting point to obtain candidate binders that could work as inhibitors 
of PADI4, and perhaps also other members of the family of PADI pro­
teins. In the case of the Polycomb peptides, based on the enzymatic 
results (Fig. S5), we showed that the selected polypeptides were sub­
strates of PADI4.
Although from the point of view of fluorescence the apparent affinity 
constant measured are not very different from each other (Table 2), the 
affinity constants measured by ITC (Table 3) and those estimated from 
BLI (Table 4) indicate that the affinities of the citrullinated peptide were 
smaller than those of the corresponding counterparts (i.e., larger Kd 
values). These findings about how citrullination affects binding to 
PADI4 agree with those previously found for other citrullinated peptides 
with no relationship to RYBP or RING1B [61,62,72], and suggest that 
once the substrate protein is citrullinated, PADI4 tends to dissociate 
from the post-translationally modified protein, which is a phenomenon 
observed in other enzymes for their substrate and the products of the 
reaction [74]. Furthermore, this dissociation of the modified protein 
occurs much faster than for the wild-type peptides (Table 4), which 
agrees, in broader terms, with the kinetic parameters of the dissociation 
reaction also measured for other citrullinated peptides [61,62,72].
It is interesting to note that, in a previous work of ours, the Arg53 of 
RYBP was predicted to be the residue prone to citrullinate [36]; how­
ever, the close proximity of both Arg53 and Arg56 in our structural 
model of the corresponding RYBP region bound to the catalytic residue 
Cys645 of PADI4 prompted us to study the two citrullinated peptides. In

measured by ITC (Table 3) or BLI (Table 4). The latter also suggested 
that the velocity of separation from PADI4 was much larger for the 
modification occurring at Arg56. Thus, citrullination at Arg56 appeared 
to be less favored from both a thermodynamic and kinetic point of view.
We also investigated whether the citrullinated peptides, despite their 
smaller affinity for PADI4, could be also capable of binding to PADI4 and 
aiming at its active site, similarly to the wild-type polypeptides. Our 
molecular docking results, obtained by using a blind simulation proto­
col, showed that the target hot-spot of all the peptides was the catalytic 
site of PADI4 rather than an allosteric site. Although the confidence 
scores of the docking poses were moderate, the values found were more 
favorable than those we had recently reported for other wild-type and 
citrullinated peptides of comparable size, all of them docking to PADI4 
[61,62,72]. The confidence scores obtained for the two C-RING1B and 
RYBP wild-type peptides were similar to those obtained for the cit­
rullinated ones (Tables 5 and ST5). This indicates that the simulation 
does not have a sufficient sensitivity to detect any variations due to the 
small difference in the peptides (citrulline and arginine differ by a single 
atom), similarly to fluorescence (Table 2), and at variance with both ITC 
(Table 3) and BLI (Table 4).
Consistently with the stochasticity of the diffusion-based docking 
algorithm used and the variability observed in the results of different 
simulation runs, we interpret the docking poses obtained as late-stage 
intermediates along the PADI4/peptide binding process rather than 
‘ideal’ binding configurations (i.e., they were not equivalent to struc­
tures that might be obtained by crystallography or cryo-electron mi­
croscopy). Furthermore, as previously hypothesized [61,62], such 
variability may suggest an induced-fit association mechanism for the 
corresponding Polycomb protein regions, which would limit the 
conformational landscape in the process of recognition and binding to 
PADI4.
We pinpoint that in our previous works, we have shown that the 
interaction between RING1B and PADI4 [37], and between RYBP and 
PADI4 [36], was occurring in different cancer cell lines. We do not know 
yet whether such interactions, and citrullination of the corresponding 
arginines (Arg231 for C-RING1B, and Arg53 or Arg56 for RYBP), are 
important for cancer development, but the fact that the corresponding 
wild-type peptides bind to the active site of the enzyme suggest that 
these peptides, once they are properly delivered into the cancer lines, 
could be used as potential inhibitors of such interactions.
5. Conclusions
To sum up, we have shown that isolated peptides encompassing the 
binding site of two proteins belonging to the Polycomb system, RING1B 
and RYBP, were also substrates of the enzyme PADI4. Our study may 
constitute one more step towards the understanding of complex epige­
netic mechanisms, because the Polycomb system intervenes in crucial 
pathways that include development during embryogenesis, preservation 
of cell plasticity for specialization, and safeguarding of cell identity 
during growth. All these mechanisms are crucial in preventing misregulation or mutations that might lead to cancer. The effect of cit­
rullination in this context is still poorly understood, and studies on this 
irreversible PTM seem to be still too sparse to get a general picture. In 
this context, our study constitutes one more indication of involvement of 
PADI4 (and possibly other PADI isoenzymes) on physiopathological 
process involving Polycomb proteins. Although it might be too early to 
draw conclusions on all the possible implications, we hope that this 
could have an impact to reveal future opportunities in cancer therapies.
